Fragile Entanglement

In this tutorial, you will learn about entanglement: how two quantum particles each give random answers on their own, yet show a shared pattern when measured together.

  1. Learn: Introduces entanglement through simple comparisons and measurement results. Compare independent particles with an entangled pair, see how their measurement results differ, and learn how they can detect a tiny change called phase.
  2. Hulk Smash: Use what you learned to help Hulk land on the target and SMAAAAAASSSSSH.
  3. Explore: Play with different noise and shot settings to observe how noise affects measurement precision.

Introduction to Entanglement

What is a qubit?

A qubit, short for quantum bit, is one unit of quantum information. It can be stored in a physical system such as an atom. A normal computer bit stores 0 or 1. When we measure a qubit, we also get one result: 0 or 1.

What makes a qubit different is how we can prepare it before measurement. It can be in a superposition: a quantum combination of its 0 and 1 states. This does not mean we read both answers at once. We still get just one answer each time; the preparation and measurement setting determine how likely each answer is. Repeating the experiment with newly prepared qubits reveals those probabilities.

What will we learn about entanglement?

Imagine two independently tossed coins. Knowing one result does not tell you the other. We begin with two independent qubits whose results behave that way. Then we compare them with an entangled pair: two qubits prepared in a shared quantum state. Each result can be random, while their results together follow a strong pattern.

Matching results alone do not explain entanglement. Two sealed envelopes can contain matching cards without anything quantum happening. In Learn, we compare ordinary matching with an entangled pair, then change the measurement setting while keeping the preparations unchanged. The counts of 00, 01, 10 and 11 show how these preparations respond differently. For example, 01 means the left qubit gave 0 and the right gave 1.

How does this connect to Hulk Smash and Explore?

Qubits can also act as sensors for a tiny change called phase—an angle within a repeating cycle, like the position of a clock hand. We estimate that angle from many measurement results. Entanglement can make the sensor more sensitive, but disturbance from the surroundings, called noise, can weaken the benefit.

In Hulk Smash, choose Independent or Entangled sensors to help Hulk correct his jump and land on a target. In Explore, compare measurement precision as you change the number of sensing qubits. Hulk Smash uses three sensing qubits; Explore compares one through six.

What happens when I press Run quantum experiment?
  1. Qollab sends one batch to your selected backend. A simulator calculates the circuit outcomes on a classical computer. A hardware backend runs operations and measurements on physical qubits. The selected backend name appears below.
  2. Each circuit runs 400 times. Each repetition, called a shot, starts with freshly prepared qubits. We collect the resulting 0s and 1s into counts. There are 78 circuits in the batch.
  3. The results power all three views. Learn reveals the measured pair results. Hulk Smash uses phase estimates from its own measured circuits. Explore receives an initial comparison plot. Revealing a recorded result does not send another job.
  4. Learn unlocks after success. If the first run fails, the lessons remain locked until results load successfully. Returning to Intro later keeps your current lesson and results.

On IonQ, this batch uses the one-job allowance for the current Qollab run. You can keep using the loaded results. To request a new batch, stop and restart the project in Qollab. Replaying a game uses its recorded results.

What changed—and what stayed the same?

We kept the pair preparations the same and changed the measurement direction for fresh pairs. The ideal prediction: the arranged matching pair loses its agreement in the second direction, while the entangled pair keeps it. Compare that prediction with your backend’s counts. That is why matching once is not the whole quantum story.

Each entangled result was still random. Entanglement gives the pair a shared quantum pattern; it does not let you choose a result or send an instant message.

The ideal predictions may differ from a noisy backend’s counts. An entangled particle does not send a message to its partner, and you cannot choose its random result. Matching results in one direction alone do not establish entanglement.

A closer look at the ordinary matching comparison

The envelopes are an analogy for an answer fixed in advance. In the quantum comparison, we prepare both particles as 0 or both as 1 in the first direction, with a random choice each time. We do not prepare a coherent combination of the two possibilities. Turning the detectors changes the measurement question; it does not turn those prepared particles into an entangled pair.

The ordinary comparison is a random mixture of 00 and 11 in the first direction. In the second direction, its outcomes become independent. The entangled pair used here keeps its agreement in both directions. This illustrates the difference between these prepared states; ruling out all ordinary hidden explanations needs a fuller experiment.

The independent particles here are independently and randomly prepared, so their results are unpredictable in both directions. The measurement sensors introduced in later lessons use a different preparation.

GAME

Hulk Smash

Hulk wants to jump onto the target and smash it, but he doesn't know exactly where it is. The target sits a hidden distance to the left or right of where he would normally land, so he may miss. Your job is to choose the quantum sensor that helps him locate the target and adjust his jump: Independent (unentangled) or Entangled. Then press SMASH. Hulk measures the target's position with your chosen sensor and corrects his jump by that reading.

What controls the landing?

  • You choose the sensor. Independent uses three separate qubits that each sense the target's offset, and their readings are averaged. Entangled links three qubits into one shared state that amplifies the offset signal (the phase) three times, which makes it more precise but much more easily disrupted by noise.
  • The round sets the noise. The displayed noise level tells you how much the sensor's signal is weakened. It does not move Hulk or the target, and it is not the chance of missing. Noise changes between rounds; you cannot adjust it.
  • The target's offset is hidden. Its size and direction are encoded as a phase on the qubits. The sensor estimates it from quantum measurements.
  • The reading decides the landing. Hulk shifts his jump by exactly the sensor's reading. A reading close to the true offset puts him on target; an inaccurate reading makes him miss. After the smash, you see the reading, the true offset, and their difference.

Both options use three sensing qubits and the same number of measurement runs. A more precise sensor is more likely to land Hulk close to the target, but no single jump is guaranteed.

SCORING: A hit earns 1 point and lowers anger by 1 (never below 0). A miss raises anger by 3. Anger at 10 ends the game. Keep Hulk calm through 12 rounds.

Inspect the recorded measurement counts

    

      How does the game connect to the experiment?
      
The hidden push is the phase we want to measure. In Learn, phase is a small angle that changes quantum measurement probabilities. Hulk Smash represents that angle as a sideways push. Hulk needs an accurate reading of the push to correct his jump.

      
Your choice selects the quantum sensor. Independent uses three unentangled sensing qubits and averages their readings. Entangled uses three qubits in a shared state that magnifies the phase signal. Both options use the same number of sensing qubits and shots. Entanglement can help when noise is low, but its shared signal is more fragile as noise increases.

      
Measurement results determine Hulk’s correction. Running the quantum experiment in Intro collects 0/1 counts from circuits measured in two settings. We combine these counts into a phase estimate for each sensor and round. When you press SMASH, Hulk automatically uses the estimate from your selected sensor. The reading stays hidden until you commit.

      
A close estimate lands on target. Hulk hits when the estimate differs from the true phase by at most 0.05 radians. A larger difference makes him miss. The result shows both values so you can see why he landed where he did.

      
Uncertainty explains reliability over many attempts. Smaller phase uncertainty means readings tend to cluster more tightly around the true angle. It does not guarantee a hit: a noisy sensor can get lucky once. Compare several rounds to understand the effect of noise and your sensor choice.

      
The jump is a game illustration; the sensor readings come from the experiment’s recorded results. Each round has its own measurements. Replay uses the same results; run the experiment again for new measurements.
